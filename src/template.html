How Stretched Each Market Is: Prices Versus Rents

Each bar compares a metro's home prices to its rents today against that metro's own 1990 to 2019 average (FHFA home price index divided by the BLS rent index). +30% means a dollar of rent buys about 30% less house than it typically did there. This measures prices relative to rents, not prices relative to their own history, so a metro can rank low here (Detroit, where rents rose almost as fast as prices) even when its prices are far above their past trend. Moving the norm to 2000 to 2019, which includes the 2000s bubble, lowers most bars by 5 to 14 points but barely changes the ranking. The reversion price paths assume this premium shrinks over time, so prices grow more slowly than rents until it closes; the bigger the bar, the bigger that drag. Click a bar to pick that market. How the Model Works

The model runs month by month. On day one, the buyer pays the down payment and closing costs; the renter invests that same amount. Each month both households spend the same total: the buyer pays principal, interest, property tax, insurance, maintenance, HOA, and PMI (less any tax savings from itemizing), and the renter pays rent and renter's insurance. Whoever has the cheaper month invests the difference at the investment return. Dividends are part of that return; they are taxed each year at the capital gains rate and the rest is reinvested.

Property tax is a percentage of the home's market value at the start of each year, so it rises as the home appreciates, unless you set a cap on taxable value growth (California's Prop 13 limits it to 2% a year from the purchase price, never above market value). Insurance and maintenance start as percentages of the purchase price and then grow with inflation, because they track what it costs to repair or rebuild the structure, not the value of the land under it. Rent rises at the rent growth rate. PMI applies only below 20% down and stops when the scheduled balance reaches 78% of the purchase price or at the midpoint of the loan term, whichever comes first. The tax benefit is the real one: your marginal rate times how far mortgage interest plus property tax (with the SALT cap) push you above the standard deduction, compared to what you'd itemize as a renter.

Home prices follow one of two paths. With a set rate, they grow at the appreciation input every year. With revert to norm, the model starts from today's price-to-rent ratio (price divided by a year of rent) and closes the gap to the market's long-run norm by half every half-life, so prices grow with rents times the shrinking gap. The presets come from the data: across 18 metros, the price-to-rent gap closed by half about every 11 years on 1990 to 2019 data and every 24 years including the 2020s; the 2000s booms unwound in 4 to 8 years. Gaps also tend to widen further before they close, which this smooth path does not capture.

At the sale, the buyer gets the home's value minus selling costs, the remaining loan, and tax on any gain above the home sale exclusion, plus their own portfolio after capital gains tax. The renter gets their portfolio after capital gains tax.

Simplifications: returns are smooth (real markets are volatile, and stocks far more than an index of home prices); no refinancing; property tax tracks market value (some states cap assessments); dividends are taxed at the capital gains rate (true for qualified dividends) and the remaining investment gains are taxed once at the sale; the SALT phase-down above $505k of income and the income-limited PMI deduction are not modeled; all buyer closing costs are treated as cost basis. The model also assumes the renter actually invests the savings, which is the behavioral assumption that most often fails in real life.

  5. How fast gaps close. In the 2000s bust, price-to-rent fell from peak to trough in 4 to 8 years (median 6) across 19 metros. Gaps can also widen further before closing, which a smooth path does not capture.
